## The problem

When an IT admin asks Copilot how to configure something across a fleet of company laptops, a wrong answer has a cost, because they act on it. The alternatives they had were a web search that returns forum threads and stale docs, or a general-purpose chatbot that sounds equally confident whether or not it's right.

For commercial customers, "better than search" has to mean two concrete things. The answer comes from a source the customer can trust, and someone has measured that it's correct often enough to rely on.

## The calls I made

**Ground answers in sources we can stand behind.** I worked on grounding Copilot's help answers in trusted sources, so responses come from content we can vouch for instead of whatever ranked well on the web.

**Score two things, not one.** I defined the LLM-as-judge evaluation strategy for Copilot's help experiences: the methodology, the scoring criteria and the success thresholds. Every answer was scored on:

- **Tool-invocation accuracy.** Did Copilot call the right source or tool for this question?
- **Response quality.** Is the answer correct, complete and safe to act on?

A wrong tool call and a wrong answer need different fixes, so scoring them separately told the team where to look.

**Make the judge earn its place.** A judge model is only useful if it agrees with the people it stands in for, so I calibrated it against domain-expert assessments and ran offline evals with Applied Science before trusting it to gate a release.

**Turn "it feels better" into a threshold.** Changes had to clear the success thresholds to ship, which gave the team a number to argue about instead of an impression.

## Outcome

Session success rate rose **7 percentage points**, and every later change to sources or prompts had to pass an eval calibrated to expert judgment.

## What I learned

Grounding and evals are the same project. Adding trusted sources without measuring the answers just moves the hallucination somewhere harder to see, and measuring answers without fixing the sources only gives you a precise number for a bad experience.
