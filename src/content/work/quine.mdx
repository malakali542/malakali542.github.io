## The problem

Biology runs on slow loops. A scientist proposes something, waits weeks for an experiment, and spends scarce samples on the answer even when it's no. Quine is Microsoft Research's attempt to shorten those loops, with two parts built together: a **world model of biology** that learns shared representations across genomics, proteins, chemistry, RNA and cell state, and imaging, and a **harness** that connects the model to scientific tools, the literature, the wet lab and the scientists using it.

The [public announcement](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/introducing-quine-an-ai-research-system-designed-for-the-complexity-of-biology/) shows what that looks like when it works. With collaborators at the Broad Institute, Quine ranked thousands of compounds by their predicted ability to shift pancreatic cancer cells between states. In the lab, the top-ranked compounds produced the largest intended shifts, and narrowing the search took one weekend.

## What I own

The model is a large language model post-trained across five biological modalities, with mid-training followed by reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). I own the decisions that sit around the training runs: which capabilities matter, how we'll know the model has them, what data and compute that takes, and when a checkpoint is ready for scientists. That covers the capability roadmap, the evals, acquisition of 40+ internal and external datasets, 50,000+ GPU-hours of compute, the research workbench scientists use, and biosafety.

## The calls I made

### Write evals from what scientists actually do

I ran evaluation programs with research labs and **five Fortune 500 pharma R&D partners**, on drug discovery and preclinical work like cell type annotation and perturbation prediction. Their workflows showed us capabilities we weren't measuring yet. Each real-world failure became new mid-training data or a new RL task, and benchmark pass rates rose **15% across three model iterations**. Beyond those programs, I've negotiated **10+** evaluation partnerships with academic institutions and Fortune 500 companies to use our research tools and models in real-world deployments, and some have produced novel candidates for drug discovery.

Competitive analysis fed the same roadmap. Studying Google's AI co-scientist, AWS's scientific discovery work and Anthropic's science efforts set our data acquisition strategy and the synthetic data strategy behind **4+ shipped capabilities**.

### Fix the data before scaling RL

For the team's first RLVR program I designed the verifiable-reward task set, the evaluation framework and the learning curriculum. Gaps in the training data and preprocessing were holding it back, and fixing them improved held-out task accuracy **12%**. I also led the team's first adversarial and held-out evaluation program, with stress tests built to surface unexpected behavior and a repeatable way to track failure modes across model iterations.

### Let the evals move the compute

I direct **50,000+ GPU-hours** across mid-training and RL, writing the success criteria for each experiment and moving compute toward the capability gaps the evals expose. Promotion gates agreed across **30+ researchers** turn benchmark and held-out results into go or no-go decisions, and the data and eval dashboards I built help those researchers catch regressions.

### Judge the product by time to an experiment

I'm the product lead for the multi-agent research workbench on top of the model, which turns literature review, data analysis and agentic workflows into wet-lab-ready experimental plans. Workflow research with scientists decided which connectors and skills we built, and I defined how we benchmark the system: benchmarking pipelines, core evals and custom gym benchmarks for agent capabilities. It reached **100+ scientists across four labs** and cut the time from research question to experimental plan by **30%**.

I led deployments of the research system to four academic labs and of the model to four enterprises, with telemetry, evals and benchmarks I defined with the researchers involved, and those feedback loops cut the time to generate and rank candidates by **15%**. For enterprise use I specified the protected managed environments, the access APIs and the evaluation requirements. I also run the wet-lab validation loop with four contract research organizations, so predictions get tested in assays and the results come back as training and evaluation data. That loop surfaced novel candidate pathways in pancreatic cancer and metastasis, now prioritized for wet-lab validation.

### Make biosafety a release gate

I'm the program and product lead for biosafety. With policy, research, engineering and Microsoft's internal safety board, I set up release criteria and a review process now used across Microsoft research teams and **20+ models**. The review caught a case where model outputs could be combined with public data to enable misuse, which changed a release decision and led to new output filtering.

## What I've learned

The eval is the real product spec. Whoever writes it decides what the model gets better at, so I write evals from what scientists actually do instead of from what's easy to grade. Compute should follow evidence, because the fastest way to waste 1,000 GPU-hours is to keep training toward a target your evals stopped believing in. And scientists rarely ask for a smarter model. They want a shorter path to an experiment worth running, which makes this a product problem as much as a research one.
