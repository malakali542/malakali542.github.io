At Uber I was a software engineer in New York on two teams with very different users. On **UberX Share**, the user was a rider deciding whether to share a car with a stranger. On **MapStore**, an internal service for map data, the user was another engineer waiting on a query. The work looked different on each team, but both came down to making a system easier and faster for the person on the other end.

## UberX Share

Uber paused shared rides in 2020 and spent the time after that rebuilding them from rider and driver feedback. The new UberX Share came with a clear trade. You might pick up a co-rider heading the same way, and in return you get an upfront discount, up to 20% off if you're matched, and arrive no more than 8 minutes later than a regular UberX. It launched across U.S. cities including New York ([Uber Newsroom](https://www.uber.com/us/en/newsroom/uberx-share/)).

### Riders cancelling rides they didn't understand

On paper the trade is simple. In the app, a rider who hadn't understood it would see an unexpected stop or a stranger getting in, and cancel. A lot of those cancellations had nothing to do with price or wait time, because they came from riders who didn't know what they'd booked. Every one of them also cost a driver a trip and a co-rider a match.

I built rider flows to cut those cancellations, including the first-time-user experience for UberX Share in the Rider app, so the trade is explained before the ride instead of discovered during it. I also ran A/B experiments on the opt-in funnels and turned what the research and data showed into feature changes that shipped, which lifted shared-ride adoption **4.5%**.

### Changing flows without waiting on an app release

Testing changes to rider flows was slow, because every change had to wait for an app release and then for people to update. I onboarded server-driven UI to our microservice, so the backend describes what a screen shows and we could change and test a flow without shipping a new version of the app. Once it was working for us, I pitched it to the wider shared rides and ride matching org so other teams could move the same way.

### Rolling out market by market

Shared rides come with different rules in different places, so the rollout couldn't be a single switch. I led it across **4 markets**, working with legal, policy and operations so the technical implementation matched each market's regulations.

## MapStore

Engineering teams across Uber build internal tools on MapStore's map data, so those tools are only as fast as its queries. Queries returned entire result sets in a single response, and the tools on top of them loaded slowly as a result, a cost every team paid on every request.

I redesigned MapStore's service interface and query architecture. Responses that used to return everything became paginated, so a tool could get the first page back quickly instead of waiting on the whole set. I rewrote the slowest SQL queries and trimmed payloads to the fields tools actually used, so the tools built on MapStore loaded faster. Average query time dropped **40%** for the teams that depend on it.
