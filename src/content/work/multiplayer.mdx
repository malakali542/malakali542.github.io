## Why single-player AI wasn't enough

AI assistants are built for one person at a time. Each session starts from a blank prompt, what one person works out stays in their own history, and a fix that helps one teammate never reaches the next. Science is done in teams, so a lab that works this way gets faster one person at a time and never together.

## How it works

On this system you can see your teammates' sessions and build on them instead of starting from scratch. Anyone using it can also change its code base, so whoever runs into a gap can close it themselves. And the system learns from how the team uses it, improving itself in a weekly cycle, so it gets better the more people work in it.

## What I did

I own its product features and the vision for where it goes. To work out what scientists needed from a shared system, I ran user studies and observation studies with them and mapped the deterministic parts of their workflows, the steps that run the same way every time. From there I ran rapid experiments and built and shipped **5+ collaboration features** myself, without engineering support.

I also defined how we know it's working, with session success rate, LLM-as-judge evaluation and regression tracking as the measures, and I managed the system's data privacy boundaries as it spread across teams.

## From one team to the org

It started as one team's project. We rolled it out across the whole org, more than 1,000 people, and in its first month it reached **35% adoption**, with **300+ users** across 5+ teams running **more than 100,000 sessions**.

## What I'm working out

Single-player AI made each of us faster, and the question I'm working on now is what makes a team faster. Most of it comes down to defaults and trust: which work should be shared automatically, and how a team trusts a change someone else made to a system everyone depends on.
